Example Datasets

Throughout these lectures we will use three recurring example datasets:

  1. Boston house price data
  2. Columbus crime data
  3. EU regional data collected at NUTS2 level

These datasets represent the main structure of lattice data: each observation belongs to an areal unit and has both geometry and attributes.

The Columbus data are associated with neighbourhood-level crime analysis.

The EU NUTS2 data use the Nomenclature of Territorial Units for Statistics. NUTS2 regions are widely used by Eurostat and other European Union bodies.

Any spatial dataset includes:

  • geometric characteristics of the spatial units;
  • spatial coordinates or polygon geometries;
  • an index for the geometries;
  • attributes associated with each spatial unit.

These attributes may be economic, demographic, health, crime, educational or environmental variables.

Analysing Spatial Lattice Data

In lattice data analysis, the units are areas.

Examples include:

  • census tracts;
  • wards;
  • municipalities;
  • provinces;
  • districts;
  • grid cells;
  • countries;
  • NUTS regions.

The key statistical problem is that values observed in nearby areas may not be independent.

Introduction

Tobler’s first law of geography:

Everything is related to everything else, but close things are more related than things that are far apart.

From this definition, we need to identify:

  • what we mean by spatial dependence;
  • how we measure spatial dependence;
  • who the neighbours of each unit are;
  • what the strength of the relationship is between neighbouring units.

To do this, for each unit we need:

  1. a list of neighbours;
  2. a spatial weights matrix.

Spatial Dependence

Spatial dependence in a collection of sample observations means that one observation associated with location \(i\) depends on observations at other locations \(j\), where \(i \neq j\).

Spatial dependence may occur for two broad reasons:

  1. Measurement error and data collection

    Geographic units may be constructed in ways that create dependence. For example, administrative boundaries may not align with the underlying social or environmental processes.

  2. Substantive spatial process

    The spatial dimension of the research problem may matter. For example, crime in one neighbourhood may be related to crime in neighbouring neighbourhoods.

Spatial Dependence: Plain Definition

Spatial dependence reflects a situation where values observed in one areal unit depend on the values of neighbouring observations in nearby areas.

Symbolically, if \(y_i\) is the value in area \(i\), spatial dependence means that \(y_i\) may be associated with values such as \(y_j\) in neighbouring areas.

This motivates the core question:

[ j i? ]

and then:

[ j i? ]

Positive and Negative Spatial Dependence

Positive spatial dependence

Objects that are close in space tend to assume similar values.

  • high values near high values;
  • low values near low values;
  • spatial clustering of similar values.

Negative spatial dependence

Objects that are close in space tend to assume dissimilar values.

  • high values near low values;
  • low values near high values;
  • checkerboard or overdispersed patterns.

Spatial dependence can take the form of spatial correlation.

Exploring Spatial Dependence

The level of spatial dependence can be explored using graphical techniques and statistical measures.

Common tools include:

  • graphical representation of spatial dependence;
  • Moran’s I statistic;
  • Geary’s C statistic;
  • Moran scatter plot;
  • spatial correlogram;
  • variogram-style summaries.

Cliff and Ord showed that Moran’s I is often a better measure than Geary’s C in many cases, although both remain important.

Moran’s I and Geary’s C

Moran’s I and Geary’s C both account for:

  1. attribute similarity: are values similar or dissimilar?
  2. locational similarity: are the corresponding areas neighbours or close to one another?

Important distinction:

  • Moran’s I focuses on similarity.
  • Geary’s C focuses on dissimilarity.

They are interpreted in opposite ways.

Moran’s I and Geary’s C are strongly linked to the spatial weights matrix.

With correlograms and variograms, we step away from a single weights matrix and look at distances between pairs of observations.

Global Moran’s I Statistic

Moran’s I is a global measure of spatial autocorrelation.

A common row-standardized form is:

[ I = , ]

where

  • \(\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{y}-\bar{y}\mathbf{1}\) is the mean-centred variable;
  • \(W\) is the row-standardized spatial weights matrix;
  • \(W\mathbf{z}\) is the spatial lag of \(\mathbf{z}\).

More generally, Moran’s I can be written as:

[ I = {_{i=1}{N}(y_i-y)2}, ]

where

[ W_0 = _ij w{ij}. ]

Global Moran’s I: Assumptions and Interpretation

Global Moran’s I can be seen as one overall measure of spatial autocorrelation.

We can use this statistic when two assumptions are satisfied or appropriately handled:

  1. Constant mean

    We usually deal with this by mean-standardizing the variable.

  2. Constant variance

    The denominator standardizes by the total variation in the variable.

The value of Moran’s I depends strongly on the chosen weights matrix. Therefore, Moran’s I values from different datasets or different weights matrices should not be compared naively.

A standardized \(Z\) value is more comparable because it accounts for the expected value and variance under the null model.

How to Construct a Weights Matrix

For geographic data, there are two main families of spatial weights:

Spatial weights based on boundaries

  • Rook contiguity
  • Bishop contiguity
  • Queen contiguity

Spatial weights based on distances

  • \(k\)-nearest-neighbour connectivity
  • critical cut-off connectivity
  • inverse distance connectivity
  • distance-decay functions
  • combinations of several criteria

Boundary-Based and Distance-Based Weights

Boundary-based weights define two observations as connected if their regions share a boundary or vertex.

These are most relevant for polygon data, such as wards or census tracts.

Distance-based weights use distances between representative points, often centroids.

These may be:

  • binary, such as neighbours within a threshold distance;
  • continuous, such as inverse distance weights;
  • row-standardized, so that each row sums to 1.

Defining Spatial Neighbours: Boundary Based

Neighbourhood relationships can be defined using contiguity.

In discrete-parameter random fields, a simple definition of neighbourhood is based on adjacency between polygons.

Two polygons indexed by \(i\) and \(j\) are neighbours if they share a boundary relation.

The common forms are:

  • rook;
  • bishop;
  • queen.

Boundary-Based Neighbour Structures

The result of any spatial econometric or spatial autocorrelation analysis can depend on the chosen topology and neighbouring structure.

Therefore, it is wise to test the robustness of results using several definitions of neighbourhood.

A binary contiguity matrix \(C\) has elements:

[ c_{ij} = \[\begin{cases} 1, & \text{if areas } i \text{ and } j \text{ are neighbours},\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}\]

]

Usually:

[ c_{ii}=0. ]

Spatial Weights Based on Boundaries

Suppose we have five regions and want to construct a \(5\times5\) binary matrix \(W\).

Each element \(w_{ij}\) records whether region \(i\) is connected to region \(j\).

For example:

  • \(w_{12}=1\) if region 1 is contiguous with region 2;
  • \(w_{34}=1\) if region 3 is contiguous with region 4;
  • \(w_{ij}=0\) otherwise.

For undirected contiguity, the matrix is symmetric:

[ w_{ij}=w_{ji}. ]

Rook Contiguity

Rook contiguity defines \(w_{ij}=1\) for regions that share a common side with the region of interest.

If two polygons touch only at a vertex, they are not rook neighbours.

Formally:

[ w_{ij}=1 i j ]

library(sf)
library(spdep)

# Queen = FALSE gives rook contiguity
rook_nb <- poly2nb(columbus_sf, queen = FALSE)
summary(rook_nb)

Row Standardization

A common transformation is to convert \(W\) so that each row sums to 1.

This is called a row-standardized first-order contiguity matrix, often written as \(W^s\).

For row-standardized weights:

[ w^s_{ij} = . ]

Then:

[ j w^s{ij}=1. ]

This makes the spatial lag an average of neighbouring values.

rook_listw <- nb2listw(rook_nb, style = "W")
rook_W <- listw2mat(rook_listw)
rowSums(rook_W)[1:10]

Bishop Contiguity

Bishop contiguity defines \(w_{ij}=1\) for regions that share a common vertex with the region of interest.

This means regions are neighbours if they touch at a corner.

Formally:

[ w_{ij}=1 i j ]

Bishop contiguity is less commonly used alone in applied work, but it is conceptually useful because it helps explain queen contiguity.

Queen Contiguity

Queen contiguity defines \(w_{ij}=1\) for regions that share either:

  • a common side, or
  • a common vertex.

Formally:

[ w_{ij}=1 i j ]

Queen contiguity is often the default for polygon neighbourhoods because it is inclusive.

# Queen = TRUE gives queen contiguity
queen_nb <- poly2nb(columbus_sf, queen = TRUE)
queen_listw <- nb2listw(queen_nb, style = "W")
summary(queen_nb)

More Ways to Define Neighbours

There are many more ways to proceed.

Neighbour definitions can be based on:

  • physical adjacency;
  • centroid distance;
  • travel time;
  • commuting flows;
  • river networks;
  • road networks;
  • social connections;
  • trade or migration flows.

The appropriate definition depends on the process under study.

Difference Between Queen and Rook

Rook contiguity includes only shared edges.

Queen contiguity includes shared edges and shared vertices.

Therefore:

[ . ]

This difference can affect:

  • number of neighbours per area;
  • row-standardized weights;
  • spatial lags;
  • Moran’s I;
  • Local Moran clusters;
  • regression estimates in spatial models.

Defining Spatial Neighbours: Distance Based

Distance-based neighbourhoods use distances between locations, often polygon centroids.

Two common definitions are:

\(k\)-nearest-neighbour connectivity

Area \(j\) is a neighbour of area \(i\) if \(j\) is among the \(k\) closest areas to \(i\).

Critical cut-off neighbourhood

Area \(j\) is a neighbour of area \(i\) if:

[ d_{ij} d^*, ]

where \(d_{ij}\) is the distance between areas \(i\) and \(j\), and \(d^*\) is a chosen threshold.

Distance Measures

Distances may be calculated using latitude and longitude or projected coordinates.

Examples include:

  • straight-line Euclidean distance;
  • great-circle distance;
  • travel distance;
  • travel time;
  • network distance;
  • other spatial separation measures.

Straight-line distances treat coordinates as points in a flat plane.

Great-circle distances measure distance along the surface of the Earth.

In many applications, straight-line and great-circle distances are not enough. Actual travel routes may require a transport network.

First Nearest Neighbour: Observation 1

For first nearest-neighbour connectivity, define:

[ w_{ij}=1 ]

if region \(j\) is the closest region to region \(i\).

The pairwise distances are calculated using centroids.

coords <- st_coordinates(st_centroid(st_geometry(columbus_sf)))
knn1 <- knearneigh(coords, k = 1)
nb_knn1 <- knn2nb(knn1)
nb_knn1[[1]]

First Nearest Neighbour: Observation 2

The same rule is applied to observation 2.

For each observation \(i\), find the \(j\) with the smallest distance:

[ d_{ij} = {k i} d{ik}. ]

Then set:

[ w_{ij}=1. ]

First Nearest Neighbour: Observation 3

For observation 3, again calculate all distances from observation 3 to all other centroids.

The nearest polygon receives weight 1 in row 3 of the binary neighbour matrix.

All other entries in row 3 receive weight 0, unless ties are treated specially.

First Nearest Neighbour: Observation 4

For observation 4, the first nearest neighbour is the region with minimum centroid distance from observation 4.

The row for observation 4 has exactly one non-zero off-diagonal entry when \(k=1\).

For \(k=2\), each row would have two non-zero entries.

First Nearest Neighbour: Observation 5

For observation 5, the nearest neighbour rule completes the neighbour list for the five-region example.

The resulting binary matrix may be asymmetric:

[ w_{ij} w_{ji}. ]

If a symmetric matrix is required, one may use a symmetrised version such as:

[ w_{ij}^{sym}=1 w_{ij}=1 w_{ji}=1. ]

First Nearest Neighbour: Complete Structure

The full first-nearest-neighbour matrix has one neighbour per row.

In R:

coords <- st_coordinates(st_centroid(st_geometry(columbus_sf)))
nb_knn1 <- knn2nb(knearneigh(coords, k = 1))
listw_knn1 <- nb2listw(nb_knn1, style = "W")

plot(st_geometry(columbus_sf), border = "grey")
plot(nb_knn1, coords, add = TRUE, col = "blue")

Critical Cut-Off Neighbourhood

To define neighbours within a distance, we need a distance threshold.

This threshold is often called a critical cut-off.

For example, using first nearest neighbours, calculate the nearest-neighbour distance for every observation. Then choose a threshold at least as large as the maximum of those distances.

If the maximum first-nearest-neighbour distance is 67.5045, choosing:

[ d^* = 68 ]

guarantees at least one neighbour for every centroid.

coords <- st_coordinates(st_centroid(st_geometry(columbus_sf)))
knn1 <- knearneigh(coords, k = 1)
nearest_dist <- knn1$nn.dist[, 1]
max(nearest_dist)
threshold <- ceiling(max(nearest_dist))
threshold

Critical Cut-Off Neighbourhood: Threshold 68

With a threshold of 68, two centroids are neighbours if their distance is no more than 68 units.

dnb_thresh <- dnearneigh(coords, d1 = 0, d2 = 68)
dnb_thresh_listw <- nb2listw(dnb_thresh, style = "W")

W_thresh <- listw2mat(dnb_thresh_listw)
rowSums(W_thresh)[1:10]
summary(dnb_thresh)

Because the matrix is row-standardized, each non-island row sums to 1.

Exploring Spatial Autocorrelation

Spatial autocorrelation can be explored with:

  • Moran’s I statistic;
  • Geary’s C statistic;
  • graphical representation of spatial dependence;
  • Moran scatter plot;
  • correlograms;
  • variograms.

Spatial autocorrelation tests assess whether the observed spatial arrangement departs from the null hypothesis that space does not matter.

Under the null hypothesis:

  • nearby areas do not affect one another;
  • there is independence or spatial randomness.

Under the alternative:

  • high values may be surrounded by high values;
  • low values may be surrounded by low values;
  • high values may be surrounded by low values, and vice versa.

Some Notation

To simplify notation, let:

  • \(N\) be the number of areas in the sample;
  • \(i\) and \(j\) be any two areal units;
  • \(y_i\) be the value of the variable of interest in area \(i\);
  • \(\bar y\) be the sample mean;
  • \(z_i = y_i - \bar y\) be the mean-centred value;
  • \(w_{ij}\) be the similarity or spatial weight between locations \(i\) and \(j\);
  • \(W\) be the spatial weights matrix;
  • \(W^s\) be the row-standardized weights matrix.

The spatial lag for area \(i\) is:

[ (Wy)_i = j w{ij}y_j. ]

Global Measures: Moran’s I Statistic

Moran’s I combines attribute similarity and location similarity into one measure for the whole spatial pattern.

The global statistic can be written as:

[ I = {_{i=1}{N}(y_i-y)2}. ]

If \(W\) is row-standardized, then often:

[ W_0 = N. ]

In that case the expression simplifies to:

[ I = {_{i=1}{N}z_i2}. ]

Moran’s I Expected Value and Variance

Under a spatial random process and for mean-centred data, the expected value of Moran’s I is:

[ E[I] = -. ]

This is slightly negative, not exactly zero.

As \(N\) becomes larger:

[ E[I] . ]

To test whether the observed statistic is unusual, we use a standardized statistic:

[ Z_I = . ]

The variance depends on the weights matrix and the assumptions used for the null distribution.

Comparing Moran’s I

In principle, there are two main approaches to testing observed Moran’s I values for significant departure from zero spatial autocorrelation:

  1. Random permutation test

    Randomly permute the observed values over the spatial units many times and compare the observed statistic to the simulated null distribution.

  2. Approximate sampling distribution

    Use the expected value and variance of Moran’s I to compute a standardized test statistic.

Moran’s I Statistics

A value of Moran’s I that exceeds its expected value indicates positive spatial autocorrelation.

A value of Moran’s I below its expected value indicates negative spatial autocorrelation.

Remember:

  • Moran’s I is a global measure;
  • it summarizes the whole map with one number;
  • it does not identify exactly where clusters occur.

The compact matrix expression is:

[ I = . ]

Where:

  • \(\mathbf{z}\) is the centred attribute vector;
  • \(W\) is the row-standardized weights matrix;

Moran Scatter Plot: \(Y_s\) vs Lagged \(Y_s\)

The Moran scatter plot shows:

[ Y_s WY_s. ]

The slope of the fitted line is Moran’s I when the weights matrix is row-standardized and the variable is centred/standardized appropriately.

lagY_s <- lag.listw(dnb_thresh_listw, Y_s)

abline(h = 0, v = 0, lty = 2)

Moran’s I Plot with moran.plot()

The moran.plot() function in R provides a convenient Moran scatter plot.

moran.plot(columbus_sf$CRIME, dnb_thresh_listw,

           xlab = "Crime",

The function can also help identify influential observations using standard linear-model influence diagnostics.

Moran Scatter Plot Quadrants

The plot is partitioned at the mean of the variable and the mean of its spatial lag.

The four quadrants are:

Quadrant Meaning Local pattern
High-High high value near high values positive association
Low-Low low value near low values positive association
High-Low high value near low values spatial outlier
Low-High low value near high values spatial outlier

High-high and low-low indicate positive spatial autocorrelation.

High-low and low-high indicate negative local association or spatial outliers.

Geary’s C

Geary’s C is another global measure of spatial autocorrelation.

It is based on squared differences between neighbouring values:

[ C = {_{i=1}{N}(y_i-y)2}. ]

where:

[ W_0=_ij w{ij}. ]

For row-standardized \(W\), often \(W_0=N\).

Interpretation:

  • \(C < 1\) suggests positive spatial autocorrelation;
  • \(C \approx 1\) suggests spatial randomness;
  • \(C > 1\) suggests negative spatial autocorrelation.

Spatial Correlogram

A non-parametric spatial correlogram is an alternative measure of global spatial autocorrelation.

Unlike Moran’s I and Geary’s C, it does not have to rely on one specified spatial weights matrix.

Instead, it examines correlations or covariances for pairs of observations as a function of distance.

For standardized variables, this can be expressed as a local regression:

[ z_i z_j = m(d_{ij}) + _{ij}, ]

where:

  • \(d_{ij}\) is the pairwise distance between locations \(i\) and \(j\);
  • \(m(\cdot)\) is a non-parametric function;
  • LOWESS or kernel regression is often used.

Spatial Correlogram in R

The sp.correlogram() function computes values of Moran’s I or Geary’s C for successive lag orders.

queen_nb <- poly2nb(columbus_sf, queen = TRUE)
queen_listw <- nb2listw(queen_nb, style = "W")

corr <- sp.correlogram(queen_nb,
                       columbus_sf$CRIME,
                       order = 10,
                       method = "I",
                       style = "W")
print(corr)
plot(corr)

The horizontal reference line is the expected Moran’s I under no spatial autocorrelation.

Local Measures and Tests for Spatial Autocorrelation

Local Indicators of Spatial Association, or LISA, were derived by Anselin (1995).

The motivation is to decompose global spatial autocorrelation statistics into the contribution of each individual observation.

The local Moran statistic for area \(i\) is:

[ I_i = z_i j w{ij} z_j, ]

where \(z_i = y_i - \bar y\) or a standardized version of the variable.

The summation runs over the neighbourhood set of area \(i\).

The sum of local Moran statistics is proportional to the global Moran’s I.

Why Local Statistics Matter

With large GIS datasets, global spatial autocorrelation may be only marginally useful.

Local statistics provide, for each observation, an indication of significant spatial clustering of similar values around that observation.

They are useful for identifying:

  • hot spots: local clusters of high values;
  • cold spots: local clusters of low values;
  • spatial outliers;
  • local pockets of non-stationarity;
  • locations that strongly influence global Moran’s I.

Anselin’s LISA requirements:

  1. The LISA for each observation gives an indication of significant spatial clustering around that observation.
  2. The sum of LISAs for all observations is proportional to a global indicator of spatial association.

Identification of Local Spatial Clusters

Local spatial clusters, sometimes called hot spots, may be identified as locations or contiguous sets of locations for which the LISA statistic is significant.

The general LISA can be used to test the null hypothesis of no local spatial association.

However, exact distributional results for a generic LISA may be difficult to obtain.

A common alternative is a conditional randomization or permutation approach, yielding empirical pseudo-significance levels.

Conditional Randomization for Local Moran

A conditional randomization test holds the value at location \(i\) fixed and randomly permutes the remaining values over the other locations.

For each randomized dataset:

  1. compute the local statistic \(I_i\);
  2. compare the observed statistic to the simulated distribution;
  3. calculate a pseudo-significance level.

This is repeated for each location.

The approach can also be applied to other local statistics, such as \(G_i\) and \(G_i^*\).

LISA Example: Conflict in Africa

Anselin’s LISA framework has been applied to quantitative indices of conflict and cooperation between nation-states in Africa.

In the example:

  • the map showed quartiles of a conflict index;
  • the darkest shade corresponded to the highest quartile;
  • visual inspection suggested clustering;
  • global Moran’s I was positive and significant;
  • the row-standardized weights matrix was based on first-order contiguity.

This demonstrates the connection between visual clustering, global Moran’s I and local cluster detection.

LISA Interpretation

Using the same row-standardized weights matrix as for the global measures, local indicators can be computed for each region.

The standardized local value may be obtained by subtracting its expected value and dividing by its standard deviation.

Significance may be assessed using:

  • normal approximation;
  • conditional randomization;
  • permutation-based pseudo p-values.

The result is often shown as a LISA cluster map, distinguishing:

  • high-high clusters;
  • low-low clusters;
  • high-low outliers;
  • low-high outliers;
  • non-significant areas.

Local Moran in R

The local Moran statistic can be computed using localmoran().

localmoranstatistics <- localmoran(Y_s, dnb_thresh_listw)

local_I <- localmoran(columbus_sf$CRIME, dnb_thresh_listw)

Full R Workflow: Columbus Spatial Autocorrelation

library(sf)
library(spdep)
library(spData)

data("columbus", package = "spData")
columbus_sf <- st_as_sf(columbus)

# 1. Plot the variable
plot(columbus_sf["CRIME"], main = "Crime in Columbus")

# 2. Define coordinates from centroids
coords <- st_coordinates(st_centroid(st_geometry(columbus_sf)))

# 3. Define a distance-band neighbour structure
knn1 <- knearneigh(coords, k = 1)
threshold <- ceiling(max(knn1$nn.dist[, 1]))
nb_thresh <- dnearneigh(coords, d1 = 0, d2 = threshold)

# 4. Convert neighbours to row-standardized weights
listw_thresh <- nb2listw(nb_thresh, style = "W")

# 5. Compute global Moran's I
moran.test(columbus_sf$CRIME, listw_thresh)

# 6. Compute Geary's C
geary.test(columbus_sf$CRIME, listw_thresh)

# 7. Moran scatter plot
moran.plot(columbus_sf$CRIME, listw_thresh)

# 8. Local Moran's I
local_I <- localmoran(columbus_sf$CRIME, listw_thresh)
columbus_sf$local_I <- local_I[, "Ii"]
columbus_sf$p_local <- local_I[, "Pr(z != E(Ii))"]

plot(columbus_sf["local_I"], main = "Local Moran's I")
